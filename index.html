What makes a symbol good for planning?

A symbol is good for planning when it is solver-sufficient: every value it can take leaves a conditioned problem that is cheap to solve, and the best of these solutions is the global optimum. A hybrid planner searches over symbol values and then solves the conditioned problem, so the symbol set Ψ sets the cost of both the search and the solve.

Raw mode variables make the search exponential; too few symbols leave the conditioned problem hard.

Regions where one descent is enough

Problem. Minimise a policy energy hs(x) over action chunks x in state s, as in diffusion, flow-matching or energy-based policies.

Symbols. Linear cuts u⊤x ≥ b; each symbol value ψ is a region of the action space.

τ-descent faithful. From any start inside a region, some path that never climbs more than τ above the lowest energy reached so far leads to within τ of the region's minimum.

What it buys. Keeping the best of one confined descent per region lands within τ of the global optimum, with no random restarts. In practice the regions coincide with behaviours, such as passing on the left or on the right, so fixing ψ steers the robot.

Commitments that leave an exact LP

Problem. A mixed-integer linear program over binary contact modes z and continuous states and inputs ξ, as for contact-rich manipulation with piecewise-affine dynamics.

Symbols. A few linear combinations of the modes, ψ = Wz, with a symbol basis W.

Relative affine-TU. Once ψ = Wz is fixed, the conditioned constraint matrix is totally unimodular, so its LP relaxation has an integral optimum, which is the optimum of the conditioned MILP.

What it buys. The search runs over the values of k symbols instead of all mode assignments, each candidate costs one LP, and the remaining binary variables need no branching.

Pick a region, drop a descent

The robot must pass three rows of obstacles. A trained diffusion policy scores every 16-step action chunk with an energy h, and at the start one invented symbol decides the side of the first obstacle. Click a point in either region: a descent confined to that region slides downhill, and the robot executes the chunk it finds, then keeps replanning on its own.

How are solver-sufficient symbols invented?

By counterexample-guided partition refinement (CPR). Starting with no symbols, each round verifies the current regions, repairs a failure without a new symbol when it can, and adds a symbol only for a failure that no repair removes. The same loop runs on MILPs, with a total-unimodularity oracle in place of the sampled landscape.

Q1Can solver-sufficient symbols cut computation cost?

Yes, at similar success rates. On six contact-rich manipulation tasks, three of them dexterous in-hand reorientation, symbol-guided planning runs 1.3–4.8× faster than re-solving the full MILP at every step. On five diffusion-policy tasks, two on a real robot, symbols need 1–2.4 descents to reach a near-optimal plan with 99.9% probability, where random restarts of the same policy need up to 10.3.

Dexterous in-hand cube reorientation with a Shadow Hand, three task settings. Both solvers take the same steps to the goal; each row moves one step per decision its solver returns, so playback time is proportional to solve time (one-time model setup not counted). Q2Can solver-sufficient symbols explain human-defined concepts?

Largely. We scored 127 human-defined binary factors, from nine MILP formulations of a four-pusher Push-T task, on two axes: solver necessity, how often our symbols pin the factor down, and task necessity, how much success drops without it. Factors that our symbols always pin down matter 6.5× more to the task than those pinned down less than half the time (7.2 vs 1.1).

The two rise together: the factors that the solver cannot relax away are the ones the task needs. A push at the bottom of the stem (z2) cannot be replaced, while a push at a corner of the bar (z1) can be taken over by its neighbours.

Q3Can solver-sufficient symbols be interpreted and steered?

Yes. Each invented symbol names a behaviour, and fixing its value selects it. In simulation, 94–100% of successful runs pass the first obstacle on the side that the symbol fixes; on a real robot, the symbol decides which block is sorted first.
